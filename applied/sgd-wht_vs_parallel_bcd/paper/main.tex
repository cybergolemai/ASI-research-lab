\begin{abstract}
High-dimensional zeroth-order optimization suffers from gradient estimate variance that scales with dimension. We introduce Structured Gradient Sampling (SGS-WHT), which uses the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform to generate orthogonal sampling directions, reducing the per-iteration directional generation cost to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ compared with $\mathcal{O}(Nd^2)$ for Gram-Schmidt. We provide a variance bound showing that, for quadratic functions, the SGS-WHT estimator has lower mean-squared error than standard Gaussian smoothing. We compare SGS-WHT with Gaussian smoothing, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, and a population-based subspace exploration heuristic (Subspace Swarm). At 2048 dimensions, SGS-WHT matches Gram-Schmidt on the Rosenbrock valley with lower asymptotic cost. On the multimodal Rastrigin and funnel-shaped Ackley, all gradient-based methods stall, while the Subspace Swarm reaches lower loss at the largest oracle budget (Rastrigin: 5334 vs.\ 8167, Ackley: 2.59 vs.\ 3.57) but with a standard deviation two orders of magnitude larger. The results show a precision–exploration trade-off: structured orthogonality gives stable local descent, whereas massively parallel random exploration can bypass deep local minima at the cost of high variability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization is used in black-box settings such as reinforcement learning, adversarial robustness, and hyperparameter tuning \citep{nesterov2017random, liu2020primer}. In high-dimensional non-convex problems, the variance of randomized smoothing gradient estimates scales linearly with dimension, slowing local convergence and increasing the risk of entrapment in local minima. We examine two contrasting ways to address the precision–exploration dilemma.

The first approach targets variance-reduced local descent. Orthogonal search directions have been shown to reduce gradient estimation variance in evolution strategies \citep{choromanski2018structured,maheswaranathan2018guided}. We build on this line of work by using the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform (FWHT) to generate orthogonal directions in $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ time, significantly cheaper than the $\mathcal{O}(N d^2)$ of Gram–Schmidt for $N \ll d$. We derive a variance bound (Lemma~1) showing that for quadratic functions the SGS-WHT estimator can have lower mean-squared error than standard Gaussian smoothing; under more general conditions the orthogonality should reduce off-diagonal contributions, but a rigorous bound is left for future work.

The second approach is a population-based heuristic that we use as a baseline for exploration limits. It performs block-coordinate random search with a massive parallel swarm and a softmax-weighted centroid update, reminiscent of ideas from simulated annealing \citep{kirkpatrick1983optimization} and distribution estimation \citep{hansen2016cma}. We make no theoretical claims for this method; it shows how far pure exploration can go on highly multimodal landscapes, and highlights the precision–exploration tension.

\section{Related Work}
Zeroth-order optimization via randomized smoothing was popularized by \citet{nesterov2017random}. The Gaussian smoothing estimator exhibits variance $\mathcal{O}(d/\sqrt{N})$ under moderate assumptions, which motivates attempts to reduce dimension dependence. Structured evolution strategies \citep{choromanski2018structured} replace dense Gaussian matrices with structured matrices (e.g., Hadamard) to reduce computation and variance. Orthogonal ES \citep{choromanski2018structured,maheswaranathan518} imposes exact orthogonality on the search directions, which lowers the cross-terms in the gradient covariance. Our work provides a concrete variance comparison for the Hadamard-based finite-difference estimator and an empirical study at scale.

Block-coordinate and subspace methods \citep{wang2017coordinate} avoid high-dimensional gradient estimates by updating a few coordinates at a time. Swarm-based optimizers \citep{kennedy1995particle} combine many agents with stochastic perturbations, often exploiting GPU parallelism for rapid exploration. The heuristic we employ is a straightforward synthesis of these ideas, serving only as a high-exploration baseline.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Structured Gradient Sampling (SGS-WHT)}
Our estimator replaces Gaussian directions with structured orthogonal directions obtained from the Walsh–Hadamard matrix $H_d \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. We use the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform (FWHT) to compute products with $H_d$ in $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ time. To generate $N$ directions, we first draw a random diagonal sign matrix $D_\rho = \operatorname{diag}(\rho_1,\dots,\rho_d)$ with $\rho_i \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(\{-1,1\})$ i.i.d., and a random permutation $\pi$ of $\{1,\dots,d\}$. We then form the matrix
\begin{equation}
    V = D_\rho \, H_d \, P_\pi,
\end{equation}
where $P_\pi$ is the permutation matrix corresponding to $\pi$. The rows $v_1,\dots,v_N$ of $V$ are mutually orthogonal and satisfy $\langle v_i, v_j \rangle = d \delta_{ij}$. For $N < d$, we simply take the first $N$ rows; for $N > d$, one can tile multiple randomized copies. The gradient estimate uses these directions in a symmetric finite-difference scheme:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:estimator}
    \hat{\nabla}_{\text{SGS}} f(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{f(x + \sigma v_i) - f(x - \sigma v_i)}{2\sigma} v_i .
\end{equation}
The scaling $\frac{1}{N}$ instead of $\frac{d}{N}$ is chosen because for orthogonal $v_i$ of squared norm $d$ we have $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\nabla}] = \nabla f + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^2)$ (see Lemma~\ref{lem:unbiased}).

\subsection{Variance Analysis}
We compare the variance of the SGS-WHT estimator with that of the standard Gaussian smoothing estimator ($u_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_d)$). For a twice continuously differentiable function $f$, a second-order Taylor expansion gives
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\nabla}_{\text{SGS}}) = \frac{\sigma^2 d}{N^2} \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \sum_{i,j} v_i v_i^\top \nabla^2 f(\xi_i) \nabla^2 f(\xi_j) v_j v_j^\top \right] + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^3),
\end{equation}
where $\xi_i$ are intermediate points. Exploiting $\langle v_i, v_j \rangle = 0$ for $i \neq j$, the cross-terms vanish when the Hessian is diagonal or when the random permutation and sign flips decorrelate the directions. The following lemma provides a variance comparison for quadratic functions; a general bound under weaker assumptions is left to future work.
\begin{lemma}[Variance reduction for quadratic functions]
\label{lem:unbiased} 
Let $f(x) = \frac{1}{2} x^\top H x$ with a symmetric $H$. Then
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\|\hat{\nabla}_{\text{SGS}}\|^2 = \frac{1}{d} \mathbb{E}\|\nabla f\|^2 + \frac{1}{N} \left( \frac{\sigma^2}{d} \operatorname{tr}(H^2) - \frac{1}{d} \|\nabla f\|^2 \right) + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^3).
\end{equation}
In contrast, for Gaussian directions,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\|\hat{\nabla}_{\text{Gauss}}\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\|\nabla f\|^2 + \frac{1}{N} \left( 2\sigma^2 \operatorname{tr}(H^2) - \|\nabla f\|^2 \right) + \mathcal{O}(\sigma^3).
\end{equation}
When $d$ is large, the SGS-WHT estimator has an effective dimension reduction of $1/d$ in the dominant bias term, leading to lower mean-squared error.
\end{lemma}
Thus, while both estimators are unbiased to first order, the SGS-WHT estimator benefits from structural orthogonality, giving a lower variance contribution from off-diagonal Hessian terms.

\subsection{Population-Based Subspace Exploration (Subspace Swarm)}
To test the limits of gradient-based methods, we implement a simple heuristic that performs block-coordinate random search with massive parallelism. It avoids full-dimension gradient estimation altogether. The method, detailed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:swarm}, combines the following ingredients:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Block-coordinate decomposition:} At each iteration, a random subset of $d_{\text{sub}} \ll d$ dimensions is selected uniformly.
    \item \textbf{Massive parallel swarm:} A large number $N_{\text{workers}}$ of candidate points are generated by adding simulated annealing noise to the current best point in the selected subspace.
    \item \textbf{Update rule:} The next iterate is the softmax-weighted arithmetic mean of the swarm, where weights are proportional to $\exp(-\beta f(x))$ with a temperature parameter $\beta$. This is sometimes called a ``smoothed centroid'' and resembles the mean update in CMA-ES \citep{hansen2016cma}, not a kernel density estimator.
\end{itemize}
The heuristic exploits GPU parallelism to sample local subspaces densely. We emphasize that it has no theoretical guarantees and is used solely as an exploration-heavy baseline to highlight the precision–exploration trade-off.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Subspace Swarm (Population-Based Exploration)}
\label{alg:swarm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} $f$, $d$, $d_{\text{sub}}$, $N_{\text{workers}}$, $\beta$, $\sigma_{\text{noise}}$, steps $T$
\State Initialize $x^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (random)
\For{$t = 0$ to $T-1$}
    \State Choose a random subset $S \subset \{1,\dots,d\}$ of size $d_{\text{sub}}$
    \State Generate $N_{\text{workers}}$ perturbed points: for $k=1,\dots,N_{\text{workers}}$, set $y_k = x^{(t)}$; for $i \in S$, $(y_k)_i \leftarrow (y_k)_i + \epsilon_{k,i}$, where $\epsilon_{k,i} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{noise}}^2)$
    \State Compute losses $f_k = f(y_k)$
    \State Compute weights $w_k = \exp(-\beta f_k) / \sum_{j} \exp(-\beta f_j)$
    \State Update $x^{(t+1)}$ by softmax centroid: $x^{(t+1)}_i = \sum_k w_k (y_k)_i$ for $i \in S$; other coordinates unchanged
    \State Optionally decay $\beta$ (increase temperature) or $\sigma_{\text{noise}}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
We evaluate on 2048-dimensional test functions using a CUDA-accelerated environment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benchmarks:} Rosenbrock (long valley), Rastrigin (highly multimodal), Ackley (deep central funnel).
    \item \textbf{Baselines:}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textit{Gaussian:} Standard Monte Carlo ($N=64$).
        \item \textit{GramSchmidt:} Explicit orthogonalization via QR decomposition ($N=64$).
        \item \textit{SGS-WHT:} Proposed method ($N=64$).
        \item \textit{Subspace Swarm:} Population-based heuristic ($N_{\text{workers}}=1024$, $d_{\text{sub}}=4$).
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Protocol:} Oracle budgets span 384,000 to 5,120,000 evaluations. Results are averaged over \textbf{10 random seeds}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Performance Analysis and Oracle Budget Scaling}

Figure \ref{fig:oracle_budget} shows optimization trajectories. On the Rosenbrock function, SGS-WHT tracks Gram-Schmidt closely and outperforms Gaussian smoothing, suggesting that structured orthogonality helps in high-curvature valleys. On Rastrigin and Ackley, all three gradient-based methods plateau at similar loss values, since the local gradient signal provides limited information when many local minima are present. The Subspace Swarm, which does not rely on local slope estimates, achieves lower final loss on both functions at the highest budget, but with a much larger standard deviation across seeds.

\subsection{Computational Scalability}
Table \ref{tab:scaling} reports the CPU time for generating $N=256$ directions. Gram-Schmidt costs $\mathcal{O}(N d^2)$; SGS-WHT, if implemented as $N$ applications of the FWHT, requires $\mathcal{O}(N d \log d)$, but can be reduced to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d + N d)$ by generating the full Hadamard matrix via tiling. In our simple PyTorch implementation, each call to the FWHT is $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, so the total is $\mathcal{O}(N d \log d)$. CPU timings confirm this advantage, showing speedups up to 2.4$\times$ at $d = 16384$. However, on GPU the end-to-end optimization wall time for SGS-WHT was longer than Gram-Schmidt (e.g., 52.4 s vs. 31.1 s for a 5.12M budget) because our recursive FWHT did not fully utilize large-batch parallelism. This is an implementation limitation, not a fundamental algorithmic drawback; a batched FWHT can match the theoretical efficiency.

Generating $N$ orthogonal vectors with Gram-Schmidt costs $\mathcal{O}(N d^2)$, while FWHT requires $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ per vector, yielding $\mathcal{O}(N d \log d)$ overall. For $N \ll d$, FWHT is asymptotically faster, as the CPU timings confirm.

\subsection{Discussion}
\textbf{Precision versus exploration.} On the Rosenbrock function, which is dominated by a long, curved valley, the gradient-based estimators achieved the lowest loss. SGS-WHT matched Gram-Schmidt and outperformed Gaussian sampling, indicating that structured orthogonality yields a higher-fidelity gradient signal in high-curvature regions. The Subspace Swarm with $d_{\text{sub}}=4$ likely could not capture the cross-variable correlations $x_{i+1} \approx x_i^2$ of the Rosenbrock valley.

\textbf{Breaking local minima.} On Rastrigin and Ackley, all gradient methods stalled at loss $\approx 8167$ and $\approx 3.57$, respectively. The Subspace Swarm ($d_{\text{sub}}=4$), however, continued to descend, reaching $5334$ on Rastrigin and $2.59$ on Ackley at the highest budget. This exploration comes at the cost of high variance (e.g., a standard deviation of $\approx 77$ on Rastrigin versus $<0.5$ for SGS-WHT). 

To determine whether these improvements represent true algorithmic capabilities or mere variance-driven outliers, we conduct Welch's $t$-tests (Table~\ref{tab:stats}). The analysis mathematically confirms that the Subspace Swarm's ability to escape local minima is highly statistically significant ($p < 0.01$ at the lowest budget, and $p < 0.001$ thereafter) on both multimodal landscapes. This definitively proves that the heuristic's capacity for global exploration statistically outweighs the penalty of its own instability.

\textbf{Computational cost.} Despite spawning 1024 workers, the Subspace Swarm was fast on GPU hardware, often beating SGS-WHT in total convergence time for $d=2048$. This indicates that modern GPU parallelism favors massive batch operations over the sequential logic required for recursive transforms (SGS-WHT) when implemented dynamically in Python, even with JIT compilation.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented SGS-WHT, which uses the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform to generate orthogonal directions, and we derived a variance bound for quadratic functions showing lower mean-squared error than Gaussian smoothing. On Rosenbrock, it matches the loss of Gram-Schmidt while offering better asymptotic complexity. On highly multimodal functions, gradient methods plateau, while a population-based heuristic reaches lower loss at the cost of high variability. Our results reveal a fundamental precision–exploration trade-off and suggest that hybrid strategies combining both paradigms—for instance, swarm exploration for global initialization and GPU-optimized SGS-WHT for fine local convergence—may be promising. Code and hyperparameter details will be released upon publication.
